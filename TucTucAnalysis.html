In the special cubic model, none of the coefficients have a significant p-value for the t estimate, and some doesn’t have significant p-values for the F estimate, although the Corrected Multiple R-squared is greater than the quadratic model, indicating overfitting. Nonetheless, the anova performed showed that the special cubic model does not explain the variance significantly better than the quadratic model, indicating that the quadratic model is the best fit for the data.

Hence, substituting the \(\beta\) for the estimated coefficients, the model presents itself as:

\(PDI = 1.64x_1 + 0.92x_2 + 1.76x_3 + 0.2x_1x_2 - 6.87x_1x_3 - 4.86x_2x_3\)

\(x_1\) being Tucumã oil, \(x_2\) being Tucumã butter and \(x_3\) being Brij O10.

Here, we’re able to see that the coefficients \(\beta_{13}\) and \(\beta_{23}\) are the only ones that contribute to a lower PDI. Also, they are the coefficients that have the greatest effect on the model. Therefore, the interaction between the Tucumã butter and the Tucumã oil with the surfactant Brij O10 has great influence in bringing the PDI value down. But the coefficient by itself does not indicate the response, because it is also dependent in which direction the other components will variate. For that matter, plotting the Piepel effects directions is fundamental to understand what happens to the response once we dislocate the formulation towards a component.

PDIEff <- ModelEff(nfac = 3,
                   mod = 2,
                   dir = 1,
                   ufunc = quadMod,
                   dimensions = list(x1="Tucuma", x2="Tucuma.1", x3="Brijo10"),
                   lc = c(0.165, 0.1665, 0.165),
                   uc = c(0.5, 0.5, 0.5835)
                   )

The Piepel Effect direction plot indicates that shifting the formulation in the direction of the surfactant and away from the other components tends to form more homogenic particles, but only till the parabole formed by the surfactant effect line reaches its minimum point, after that, the particles starts a heterogenic tendency again. Plotting a ternary graph of the PDI will help to visualize in which proportions of the components the nanoparticles tend to be more homogenic.

Now, let’s plot a ternary graph to access how the PDI is molded by the components of the nanoparticle.

PDIPlot <- ModelPlot(model = quadMod,
                     dimensions = list(x1="Tucuma", x2="Tucuma.1", x3="Brijo10"),
                     lims = c(0.165, 0.5, 0.1665, 0.5, 0.165, 0.5835),
                     contour = TRUE,
                     pseudo = TRUE,
                     fill = TRUE,
                     main = "PDI TucTuc",
                     axislabs = c("Tucumã oil", "Tucumã butter", "Brij O10"),
                     axislab.offset = 0.1,
                     cornerlabs = c("", "", ""),
                     grid = TRUE,
                     colorkey = TRUE,
                     labels = TRUE,
                     label.style = "mixed"
                     )

Analyzing the ternary graph, and according to the model fitted, we’re able to see a region where the components proportions tend to generate lower PDI values. Hence, the optimal formulation, in seeking the lowest PDI, would have 27% Tucumã oil, 27% Tucumã butter and 46% Brij O10 in this part of the mixture.

\[ Size(nm) = 439x_1 + 1044x_2 + 748x_3 - 238x_1x_2 - 2042x_1x_3 - 3742x_2x_3 \] The coefficients demonstrate that the components by themselves tends to contribute to a higher size, whereas the interaction between the components tends to contribute to a lower size, with the interaction of the lipids with the surfactant having the greatest coefficient in module, indicating a strong effect towards reducing the size.
